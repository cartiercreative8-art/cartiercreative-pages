The refrigerant deadline moved. Here's the short version.

EPA dropped the January 2026 install deadline for older R-410A systems. What changed, what didn't, and what to tell customers who ask.

By Carter Birky, Cartier Creative

If you run an HVAC shop, refrigerant rules have been a moving target for two years. Here's where things stand this fall.

What changed

The AIM Act of 2020 is phasing down older refrigerants like R-410A. The replacements are lower global warming refrigerants, usually called A2Ls. R-454B and R-32 are the ones you see most.

Under the original EPA rule, you couldn't install a split system built before January 1, 2025 with R-410A after January 1, 2026. That put a lot of shops in a bind. A2L equipment and refrigerant were scarce and pricey in some markets, and distributors still had R-410A units on the shelf.

In May 2026, EPA finalized a rule that removed that install deadline. It took effect July 27, 2026. Residential and light commercial R-410A systems built or imported before January 1, 2025 can still be installed until the supply runs out.

What didn't change

  • No new R-410A systems are being built. The stock that exists is the stock there is.
  • The R-410A phasedown is still on. Production keeps stepping down each year, toward 15% of its baseline by 2036. Expect service refrigerant to keep getting more expensive.
  • States can be stricter. New York kept the original January 1, 2026 deadline in state law, so no R-410A installs there. California and Washington have their own rules too. Check your state before you quote a legacy unit.

What to tell customers

Homeowners hear "refrigerant ban" and panic. Or they hear nothing and get surprised by a service bill later. Either way, a clear answer builds trust. Something like this:

"Your system is fine to keep and service. If we replace it, we can look for one of the last older-style units or go with the newer refrigerant. The older one may cost less up front. The newer one will be easier and cheaper to service for years."

Then let them choose. Customers who understand the trade-off rarely come back angry.

Why this matters for your phone

Rule changes create calls. People read a headline and want to know if their AC is "illegal" now. Every one of those calls is a chance to book a tune-up or quote a replacement.

If those calls hit voicemail, they go to whoever answers.

Sources: EPA final rule in the Federal Register, May 26, 2026. Plain-language summaries from NAHB and Holland & Knight. This is a summary, not legal advice.
