The math on one missed call

Most owners guess low. Take five minutes with your own call log and see what voicemail really costs you.

By Carter Birky, Cartier Creative

Ask an owner how many calls they miss and you'll hear "a few." Ask what those calls are worth and you'll get a shrug.

Let's put numbers on it. Grab your phone and a pen. This takes five minutes.

Step 1: Count the misses

Open your call log. Scroll back one week. Count every call you didn't pick up from a number you don't know. Skip the obvious spam.

Most owners who work in the field find more than they expected. Write your number down.

Step 2: Guess how many would have booked

Not every caller is a job. Some are price shoppers. Some are wrong numbers. Be honest and be conservative. If you usually book about half the people you actually talk to, use half.

Step 3: Put a dollar figure on a job

Use your average ticket, not your best one. A drain clear. A furnace repair. A roof patch. Whatever a normal week looks like.

Step 4: Do the math

Missed calls × booking rate × average job = what walks out the door each week.

Here's a plain example. Round numbers, not anyone's real books:

  • 6 missed calls a week
  • Half would have booked, so 3 jobs
  • $300 average job
  • $900 a week. About $3,900 a month.

That's before you count the customer who would have called you every year, or sent you their neighbor.

Step 5: Ask where they went

Most people who hit voicemail don't leave a message. They call the next name on Google.

That's not a guess about your customers. Think about the last time you needed someone fast. Did you wait?

What to do with your number

If your number is small, good. You're on it. If it made you wince, the fix isn't a new hire. Start with the cheapest thing that works:

  1. A text that goes out on its own the moment you miss a call.
  2. A link so people can book a time without reaching you.
  3. Someone, or something, that answers when you can't.

Each step catches more of the calls you're already paying to get.
